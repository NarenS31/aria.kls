# ARIA and ADHD: evidence position

ARIA was partly motivated by lived experience with ADHD. That origin can guide
questions and participatory design, but it is not efficacy evidence.

## What ARIA may responsibly say now

- The project was motivated partly by lived experience with ADHD.
- The interface offers adjustable brevity, one-step prompts, visible structure,
  breaks, and learner choice.
- These are accessibility-oriented design choices available without requiring a
  diagnosis.
- Effectiveness for students with ADHD has not been tested.

## What ARIA must not say now

- detects ADHD;
- diagnoses cognitive states;
- treats ADHD;
- is clinically validated;
- is proven to improve ADHD learning outcomes;
- replaces an IEP, 504 plan, educator, psychologist, or clinician.

The CDC notes that evidence varies across school accommodations and identifies
behavioral classroom management and organizational training as better-supported
school strategies. It also emphasizes collaboration among families, educators,
and healthcare providers:
<https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/treatment/classroom.html>

## Responsible population-specific study

An ADHD-specific claim requires:

1. qualified clinical/educational collaborators and institutional review;
2. a defensible, privacy-preserving eligibility definition that does not ask
   ARIA to diagnose;
3. involvement of students and families in interface and burden review;
4. an active comparator and the same ordinary educational services;
5. adequate power for the prespecified interaction or population effect;
6. outcomes covering learning, burden, engagement, and adverse effects;
7. no removal of participants whose needs make the system look worse;
8. reporting heterogeneity rather than implying one interface fits everyone.

The initial controlled study therefore recruits by course/topic rather than
diagnosis and treats any accessibility subgroup analysis as exploratory.
